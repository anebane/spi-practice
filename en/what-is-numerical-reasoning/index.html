What is a numerical reasoning test?

Numerical reasoning practice > What is a numerical reasoning test?

A numerical reasoning test gives you data — a table or a chart — and asks a question that can be answered from it. It is not a maths exam. The arithmetic is deliberately simple; the difficulty is in reading and time.

1. What the test involves

A typical question shows a small table or chart and asks for one value: a total, a difference, a percentage, a ratio. You choose from several options, usually under a timer that works out at around a minute per question.

The data is business-flavoured — revenue by department, units sold by product, figures across a few years — but no business knowledge is required or rewarded.

2. Why it is not a maths exam

Everything you need is arithmetic you already have: adding, subtracting, multiplying, dividing, percentages, ratios. There is no algebra, no geometry, no formulae to memorise.

What is being measured is different:

  • Can you find the relevant figure among irrelevant ones?
  • Can you tell which calculation the wording is asking for?
  • Can you do it reliably when you are short of time?

People who were strong at school maths often score lower than they expect, because the skill being tested is reading under pressure rather than calculation.

3. What a typical question looks like

The table below shows quarterly revenue by department.

Retail 300 / 320 / 350 / 460
Wholesale 340 / 160 / 190 / 170
Online 480 / 200 / 230 / 230
Export 460 / 120 / 370 / 210
(columns are Q1–Q4, unit: £000s)

What is the total annual revenue of the Export department?

The arithmetic is four numbers added together. The test is whether you found the right row, read the unit, and did it in under a minute while twelve irrelevant numbers sat on the screen.

The answer is 1,160 (£000s). A full walk-through is on the data table questions page.

4. How scoring usually works

Scores are normally reported relative to a comparison group rather than as a raw mark. That has two practical consequences:

  • Getting most questions right is not automatically a good score — it depends on the group
  • Unanswered questions and wrong answers are not always treated the same way, so guessing policy can matter

⚠️ The details vary between employers and test providers, and they change. If a specific scoring rule matters to you, the only reliable source is the instructions you are given for that test.

5. Does practice actually help?

Yes, but not by making you better at arithmetic. Practice helps because it removes three specific costs:

  • Format familiarity — you stop spending the first ten seconds working out what you are looking at
  • Question-type recognition — you learn that "how many times" means one division, not a comparison of every row
  • Pacing — you learn what a minute feels like, and when to abandon a question

That is also why repeating the same fifty practice questions stops helping quickly: once you remember the answers, none of the three costs are being trained. See how to prepare.
